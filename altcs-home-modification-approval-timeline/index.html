ALTCS home modifications

How an ALTCS bathroom modification gets approved, and how long it takes

By Aging Safely Baths · Updated November 8, 2026 · 9 min read

The assessment, the physician order, the 14-day decision clock, bids, the pended authorization and the 90-day completion rule, straight from AHCCCS policy, plus what slows requests down and what to do while you wait.

Direct answer: Under AHCCCS policy AMPM 1240-I, your ALTCS health plan must approve or deny a home modification request within 14 calendar days, with one 14-day extension allowed if it needs more information, and the contractor may not take more than 90 days from approval of the final specifications to finish the work. The assessment, physician order, bids and scheduling come before and after those clocks, so a bathroom project realistically spans months, not weeks.

In short: nothing gets approved until the packet is complete. The packet is built by your case manager from a physician order, a therapist's assessment and cost documents. Once it is in, the plan has 14 calendar days to decide. After approval, bids are gathered, the winning contractor receives a pended authorization, and the work must be done within 90 days. Keep reading for each step, the things that stall it, and the safe interim fixes.

What has to happen before anyone can approve it?

AHCCCS covers physical changes to a member's home when an assessment shows the change has a specific adaptive purpose and the change is written into the service plan. Policy 1240-I lists what the assessment and documentation must include, as appropriate:

  • An order from the member's primary care provider or attending physician.
  • Documentation of medical necessity, including how the modification affects the member's ability to perform activities of daily living on their own, and whether it also helps a caregiver.
  • Documentation of cost-effectiveness, including how permanent the residence is, residential history and any earlier home modifications.
  • An assessment by a qualified professional, usually an occupational or physical therapist. A Certified Environmental Access Consultant (CEAC) assessment can be used instead.
  • At least two competitive bids from qualified residential contractors for each project, which the policy recommends for comparing cost and options.
  • If the member does not own the home, written approval from the owner.

The policy also requires alternatives to be considered first. It gives examples of acceptable ones, such as using a different accessible bathroom or bedroom, medical equipment like a transfer bench, or community resources. It is just as clear about what is not acceptable: giving bed baths instead of making the bathroom usable is named as an unacceptable alternative. If you are new to the program, our guide to asking ALTCS for a roll-in shower covers what to say in that first conversation with your case manager.

Source: AHCCCS Medical Policy Manual, AMPM 1240-I Home Modifications (effective dates 02/14/96, 10/01/17, 03/02/20).

The clocks

How long does the health plan have to decide?

Once a request is in, the policy sets deadlines. Citing 42 CFR 438.210, it says the Contractor (your ALTCS health plan) shall approve or deny a home modification request within 14 calendar days. The plan may add one 14-day extension when it can justify that more information is needed and the delay is in the member's best interest, and it must tell the member it intends to extend. A denial has to be signed by the plan's Medical Director or a physician designee.

For Tribal ALTCS members, requests go to the AHCCCS Division of Fee for Service Management, and a written decision is due within 30 days from receipt of a properly completed request. Those words matter: the clock starts when the packet is complete, not when you first asked.

The three ALTCS-EPD plans named on the AHCCCS ALTCS page are Mercy Care LTC, Banner-University Family Care LTC and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan LTC. Each runs the steps a little differently, but the 1240-I deadlines apply to all of them.

After the yes

What happens between approval and a finished shower?

AHCCCS publishes a case manager toolbox slide deck that lays out the Tribal ALTCS process. It is the clearest public picture of the paperwork, and the EPD plans follow the same shape.

  1. Packet review. The review team checks the initial packet: the medical documentation form, the Uniform Assessment tool, the Service Assessment form and the Home Modification Request/Justification form. An incomplete packet is sent back to the case manager with the reason.
  2. Nurse review. A nurse reviews the complete packet for medical necessity.
  3. Bid notice. If approved, bid notification letters go to the case manager and to contractors, who have 30 days to submit bids.
  4. Award. Bids are reviewed and award letters go to the case manager and the chosen contractor.
  5. Pended authorization. The contractor receives a pended authorization and has 90 days to complete the approved modifications. Policy 1240-I states the same limit: no more than 90 days from approval of the final specifications to completion.
  6. Completion documents. The contractor submits member sign-off and after photos. The authorization is then approved and the contractor can bill.

Two rules worth memorizing. Work must be done by a residential contractor that is a registered AHCCCS provider, in line with state and local building codes. And no Title XIX funds may be used to return a home to its pre-modification state, so the decision about what to build should be made carefully the first time.

Sources: AHCCCS Long Term Care Case Management Digital Toolbox, Home Modification Process; AMPM 1240-I.

Avoid the stall

What delays an ALTCS bathroom modification?

Almost every long wait we hear about traces back to one of these.

An incomplete packet

The toolbox is blunt: incomplete packets go back to the case manager. A missing physician order, no therapist assessment or a justification form with blanks resets the wait before the 14-day clock even starts.

Bids that do not match

Two bids that describe different showers cannot be compared. Give each contractor the same scope from the assessment: shower size, seat, grab bars, handheld shower, door and threshold.

Contractor status and permits

The contractor has to be a registered AHCCCS provider and should hold an Arizona ROC license. Local permits may be required for drain or electrical changes. Check both early; our guides on ROC licenses and Arizona bathroom permits explain how.

Ownership and residence questions

Renters need the owner's written approval. Cost-effectiveness documentation looks at how permanent the residence is, so a planned move or a home in someone else's name needs to be explained up front.

The 90-day window

Once the pended authorization is issued, the contractor is on a 90-day clock. Long lead times on a custom shower base or a contractor who takes the award and then disappears into other jobs can push a project past it. Ask how the contractor will meet it.

Changing the plan midstream

Switching from a walk-in tub to a roll-in shower after bids are in means new bids. Decide the product with the therapist before the packet goes out. The policy lists roll-in showers and accessible fixtures as examples; walk-in tubs are not typically listed.

While you wait

What should you do during the months in between?

The policy itself names the safest interim steps, because they are the alternatives a plan considers before approving construction: use another accessible bathroom if there is one, add medical equipment such as a transfer bench, and lean on community resources. A tub transfer bench, a handheld shower on a slide bar and a well-anchored grab bar can make the current tub workable for a season. Ask your case manager whether those items can be supplied as durable medical equipment while the modification request moves.

Keep one folder for the request. Note every call with the date and the person's name, ask for every decision in writing, and ask the plan for the notice when it extends the 14-day deadline. If the answer is a denial, it must carry the Medical Director's signature; the notice explains how to appeal, and the deadline on that notice is firm.

Do not start work and do not pay a deposit until the authorization is in hand. Work done before approval is work the plan did not authorize.

One date to watch: AHCCCS has posted draft changes to its HCBS Needs Tool and Extraordinary Care Review process with a planned December 1, 2026 implementation. The needs tool feeds the assessment that supports a home modification, so if your request is in progress around that date, ask your case manager whether the new tool changes anything for you.

FAQ

ALTCS timeline questions

How long does ALTCS take to approve a bathroom modification?

Policy 1240-I gives the health plan 14 calendar days to approve or deny once the request is submitted, with one 14-day extension allowed if more information is needed and the member is notified. The weeks spent gathering the physician order, therapist assessment and bids happen before that clock starts, so plan on months for the whole project.

When does the 90-day completion clock start?

At approval of the final specifications. In the AHCCCS toolbox process the contractor receives a pended authorization and has 90 days to complete the approved modifications, and policy 1240-I states the project may not exceed 90 days from approval of the final specifications to completion. Delays before that point do not count against the 90 days.

Can I choose my own contractor?

You can suggest one, and the policy recommends at least two competitive bids for each project. Any contractor doing the work must be a residential contractor that is a registered AHCCCS provider and must follow state and local building codes. Ask your case manager how your plan handles bids before you ask anyone for a quote.

Will ALTCS approve a walk-in tub instead of a shower?

The policy's examples of covered bathroom work include roll-in showers, wheelchair-accessible sinks, repositioned fixtures and specialized toilets. Walk-in tubs are not typically listed. The plan decides each case on the assessment, so ask directly, but a barrier-free shower is the request that fits the policy most naturally.

What if the request is denied or the plan misses its deadline?

A denial must be signed by the plan's Medical Director or a physician designee and comes with appeal instructions and a deadline. If the plan extends past 14 days it must notify you of the extension. Keep your notes, ask for the reason in writing, and add the missing documentation when you appeal.

Does ALTCS pay to put the bathroom back later?

No. Policy 1240-I says no Title XIX funds may be used to return a home to its pre-modification state. Owners who rent to an ALTCS member should understand that before giving written approval, and members should choose a design they will be glad to keep.
