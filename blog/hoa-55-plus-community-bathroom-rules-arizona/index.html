HOA and 55+ community rules for Arizona bathroom changes

By Aging Safely Baths · Updated October 10, 2026 · 8 min read

Arizona has more planned communities and age-restricted neighborhoods than most states. Here is what associations usually care about when you replace a tub, and how to keep install day trouble-free.

Direct answer: In most Arizona HOAs, replacing a tub or shower inside a single-family home does not need design approval, but associations often regulate contractor access, gate registration, work hours, parking and debris. Condos are different: plumbing, walls and water shut-offs can affect neighbors, so check the CC&Rs and talk to management first. Federal fair housing law also protects reasonable modifications for residents with disabilities.

Context

Why this matters more in Arizona

Drive through the Phoenix and Tucson suburbs and you pass one planned community after another, each with its own association, CC&Rs and architectural committee. Add the large age-restricted communities in the West Valley, the Southeast Valley, Pinal County, Green Valley and SaddleBrooke, plus condo and townhome complexes everywhere, and a large share of Arizona homeowners live under some set of community rules.

For a bathroom change, that rarely means you need permission to choose a tub. It does mean the people coming to install it may need to be registered, may be limited to certain hours, and may not be allowed to leave a dumpster or an old tub at the curb. Knowing the rules ahead of time keeps the project on schedule and keeps you on good terms with your neighbors.

Typical rules

What associations usually regulate

Every community writes its own rules, so read yours. These are the topics that come up most often for interior bathroom work in Arizona communities.

Contractor access

Gate or guard registration, a contractor badge, or a list of approved hours for vendors to enter.

Licensing and insurance

Many associations ask for an Arizona ROC license number and a certificate of insurance before work starts.

Work hours

Quiet hours, no work on Sundays or holidays, or limits on noisy work in shared buildings.

Parking and trucks

Where a delivery truck or trailer can stop, and for how long. Some streets do not allow overnight parking.

Debris and dumpsters

Rules on roll-off containers, curbside bulk trash and how quickly debris must be removed.

Exterior changes

Anything visible outside, such as a new window, vent or ramp, may need architectural approval.

Interior vs exterior: most single-family HOAs focus on what can be seen from the street. Replacing a tub with a walk-in tub or a shower is usually an interior change. If the project adds a new exterior vent, changes a window or adds a ramp, expect the architectural committee to be involved.

Shared buildings

Condos and townhomes: a different picture

If you own a condo or a townhome with shared walls, a bathroom project can affect more than your own unit. Water supply and drain lines may be shared, the water shut-off for your unit may be in a common area, and floors and ceilings may sit right above or below a neighbor's.

  • Read the CC&Rs and rules for anything on plumbing, alterations or "common elements." In Arizona, condominiums are governed by the Arizona Condominium Act and planned communities by the Planned Communities Act, both in Title 33 of the Arizona Revised Statutes, along with each community's own documents.
  • Call management early. Ask who can shut off water, how much notice neighbors need, and whether any part of the work requires approval.
  • Ask about the floor. A curbless shower may require recessing or reworking the floor, which can be limited in a unit above another unit. A walk-in tub or low-threshold shower usually avoids that.
  • Plan for noise and access. Shared hallways and elevators may need to be protected or reserved.

Sources: Arizona Revised Statutes, Title 33 (Chapter 9, condominiums; Chapter 16, planned communities).

Your rights

Fair housing and reasonable modifications

Federal fair housing law gives people with disabilities the right to make reasonable modifications to their homes when the change is needed for full use and enjoyment of the home. HUD and the Department of Justice explain in a joint statement that this generally means a structural change, usually made at the resident's expense, and that housing providers, which can include associations in some situations, may not unreasonably refuse it. Arizona has its own fair housing law with similar protections.

What does that mean in practice? Most interior bathroom changes never reach that point, because associations do not regulate them. Where it matters is for changes that touch common areas or the exterior, such as a ramp to the front door or grab bars in a shared space. If your request is refused, ask for the reason in writing and consider talking with a fair housing organization or an attorney.

Source: HUD and DOJ joint statement on reasonable modifications under the Fair Housing Act.

This is general information, not legal advice. Your community's documents and the specific facts matter.

55+ communities

Age-restricted communities

Arizona's 55+ communities, from Sun City and Sun City West to Sun Lakes, Robson communities, Green Valley and SaddleBrooke, tend to have the most detailed contractor rules. That is not a bad thing. They also tend to have staff who handle contractor registration every day, so a quick call usually gets you a clear answer.

  1. Find the right office. Some communities have a recreation-center organization and a separate homeowners association. Ask which one handles contractor access.
  2. Ask for the contractor packet. Many have a short form, a license and insurance requirement and a list of allowed hours.
  3. Share the install plan. Give the date, the expected hours, the vehicle type and the fact that the old tub will be hauled away.
  4. Register the crew. Send the installer's company name and license number to the gate or guard ahead of time.
  5. Tell a neighbor. A friendly heads-up about a truck in the street goes a long way, especially in a quiet community.

Template

A short note to send your association

Copy, edit and send this to your HOA, management company or community office a week or two before install day.

Sample note: "Hello, I am planning to replace the tub in my home at [address] with a walk-in tub (or accessible shower). The work is interior only. The installer is [company], Arizona ROC license [number], and I can send proof of insurance. The planned date is [date], from about [time] to [time]. The old tub will be removed and hauled away the same day. Please let me know if there are contractor registration, parking or work-hour rules I should follow. Thank you."

Part-year residents

Snowbirds and community rules

If you are away part of the year, arrange access in advance. Some communities allow a contractor to work in an empty home only with written permission or a designated contact on site. Name a neighbor or family member who can let the crew in, check the water after the job, and lock up. Our snowbird timing guide covers when to measure, order and install around a seasonal schedule.

FAQ

HOA and community questions

Does my Arizona HOA have to approve a walk-in tub?

Usually not. Most single-family HOAs regulate exterior appearance, not interior fixtures. They often do have rules about contractor access, work hours, parking and debris, so check with your association before install day.

Is it different in a condo?

Yes. In a condo, plumbing, walls and water shut-offs may be shared or affect neighbors, so read the CC&Rs and talk with management first. Some changes may need approval, and work hours are often limited.

Can an HOA stop me from making my home accessible?

Federal fair housing law protects reasonable modifications needed by a person with a disability, generally at the resident's expense. Most interior bathroom changes never need approval, but if a request involving common areas or the exterior is refused, ask for the reason in writing and consider talking with a fair housing organization or an attorney.

What does my 55+ community usually need from a contractor?

Commonly an Arizona ROC license number, proof of insurance, gate or guard registration, and work within set hours. Ask your community office for its contractor packet.

Who removes the old tub?

We do. The old tub leaves with the crew on install day, so nothing is left at the curb or in a community dumpster.

Can the install happen while I am away for the summer?

Often, yes, if your community allows it and someone you trust can let the crew in and check the home afterward. Arrange access and any written permission ahead of time.
