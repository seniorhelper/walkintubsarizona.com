Arizona hard water and your walk-in tub

How to protect jets, heaters and drains from scale, with a care routine that takes about fifteen minutes a month.

Direct answer: Arizona water is hard. Phoenix water typically runs about 9 to 20 grains per gallon, and those minerals leave scale in walk-in tub jets, inline heaters, drain valves and door seals. Walk In Tubs Arizona recommends air jets over water jets, a monthly cleaning cycle and, where practical, a water softener to keep a tub working for years.

Why hard water matters more in Arizona

If you have lived in the Valley for a while, you already know the signs. White spots on glasses. A crust around the faucet. A showerhead that sprays sideways after a year. That is hard water, and it comes from minerals like calcium and magnesium that the water picks up on its long trip to your tap from rivers and aquifers.

Phoenix water is among the harder supplies in the country. The City of Phoenix's 2024 water report puts hardness at roughly 158 to 344 milligrams per liter, which is about 9 to 20 grains per gallon, with a typical level around 228. Anything above about 7 grains is usually called hard. Tucson water is hard too, and many smaller Arizona towns draw from wells with similar minerals.

Source: City of Phoenix 2024 water report, as summarized by tapwater.org.

A regular bathtub mostly just collects a ring. A walk-in tub has more working parts: a watertight door, jets, sometimes an inline heater, and a fast drain valve. Every one of those parts touches water, and every one can collect scale. The good news is that a little routine care, plus smart choices when you buy, keeps those parts working for years.

Where scale builds

What scale does to each part of a walk-in tub

Scale is a slow problem. It rarely breaks anything overnight, but it wears parts down quietly.

Jets

Water jets pull bath water through pipes and a pump, then push it back out. Minerals settle inside those pipes and on the jet nozzles. Over time the jets get weaker and noisier, and leftover water in the lines can hold soap and residue.

Inline heater

Many tubs have a small heater that helps keep the bath warm. Scale coats the heating surface like it coats a kettle, so the heater works harder and may wear out sooner.

Drain valve

Fast drains are a key safety feature, because you wait inside the tub while it empties. Scale on the drain valve and stopper can slow the drain or keep it from sealing fully.

Door seal

The rubber gasket on the door keeps water in. A mineral crust on the seal or the door frame can stop it from closing snugly, which can lead to small drips.

Jet choice

Air jets or water jets for hard water?

This is the single biggest hard-water decision you make when you buy. Here is the plain difference.

Water jets (sometimes called hydrotherapy or whirlpool jets) circulate the bath water itself through a pump and plumbing. They give a strong, focused massage. They also leave a little water sitting in the lines after each bath, and in Arizona that water carries minerals.

Air jets blow warm air through many small holes, which makes a gentler, bubbly feel. Because they move air, not bath water, there is less plumbing for scale to cling to. Many air systems also run a short purge after the bath to blow the lines dry.

For most Arizona homes on hard water, air jets are the lower-maintenance choice. Some people want both, and many tubs offer a combination. If you choose water jets, plan on a monthly cleaning cycle and consider a softener. Whatever jets you choose, ask your doctor whether warm water and jets are a good fit for your heart, blood pressure and circulation. Many people find warm water soothing for sore joints, but it is a personal health question.

Cleaning cycles

How a cleaning cycle works

A cleaning cycle runs cleaner through the jet system so you do not have to take anything apart. Some tubs have an automatic cycle with a button. Others ask you to fill the tub a few inches above the highest jet, add a cleaner, and run the jets for a set time. The exact steps depend on the model, so keep the owner's manual somewhere easy to find, like a kitchen drawer.

A typical routine looks like this:

  1. Fill above the jets. Use warm water, a couple of inches above the top jet.
  2. Add the cleaner. Use the product or amount your manual recommends. Avoid household bleach mixes unless the manual says they are safe.
  3. Run the jets. Usually 10 to 15 minutes is enough. Stay nearby, but you do not need to sit in the tub.
  4. Drain and rinse. Drain fully, refill with clean water above the jets, and run them again briefly to flush.
  5. Wipe down. Dry the door seal, faucet and jet faces with a soft cloth.

If bending or kneeling is hard, ask a family member to help with the first cycle so you both learn the steps. After that, most of the work is pushing a button and waiting.

Softeners

Should you add a water softener?

A water softener swaps hardness minerals for a small amount of sodium or potassium before water reaches your faucets. It protects more than the tub. Water heaters, dishwashers, faucets and shower valves all last longer on soft water, which is why many Arizona homes already have one.

A few practical points if you are thinking about it:

  • Check what you have. Many Arizona homes, especially newer ones and those in 55+ communities, already have a softener loop or a softener in the garage. Look before you buy.
  • Know the upkeep. Salt-based softeners need salt bags added every so often. If lifting a 40-pound bag is hard, ask about delivery service or lighter bags.
  • Ask about drinking water. Some people on low-sodium diets keep one unsoftened kitchen tap. Ask your doctor if sodium is a concern.
  • Consider the whole house. A softener is plumbing work, so it should be done by a licensed contractor. Our guide on checking an Arizona ROC license shows how to confirm one.

A softener is helpful, not required. Plenty of tubs work well for years on hard water with good habits. If a softener is not in the budget, choose air jets and keep up with the monthly checklist below.

A simple monthly care checklist

This takes about fifteen minutes. Pick an easy date to remember, like the first of the month, and tape the list inside a cabinet door.

If you are a caregiver, this is a good task to share. A parent can push the buttons while you handle the bending and wiping.

Part-time residents should do a full cycle before leaving for summer and again on return, since water left sitting in lines can dry into scale. Our snowbird timing guide covers what else to do before you close up the house.

  • Run the jet cleaning cycle, then a clean-water rinse
  • Wipe the door seal with a soft, damp cloth and dry it
  • Check the seal for cracks, stiffness or a white crust
  • Fill a few inches and time the drain; note if it slows
  • Clean the faucet aerator and handheld sprayer face
  • Wipe jet faces and the overflow with a mild cleaner
  • Look under the tub apron for any sign of moisture
  • Check softener salt, if you have a softener

When to call for help

Signs something needs more than a cleaning

Most hard-water issues respond to cleaning. Call for service if you notice any of these:

  • Water dripping from the door after the seal has been cleaned
  • The drain is slow even after you clean the stopper
  • Jets that will not start, or a pump that hums but does not move water
  • The heater no longer keeps water warm
  • Any water on the floor, under the apron or in a room below

Do not take panels off or reach into wiring yourself. Walk-in tubs have electrical parts near water, and repairs belong with a trained technician.

Buying a tub? Ask three questions: Are the jets air or water? Is there an automatic cleaning cycle? How do I reach the drain valve for service?

When you are choosing a tub, those answers matter as much as the color. FAQ

Questions Arizona families ask

What does hard water look like on a walk-in tub?

The first sign is usually a chalky white film on the door seal, faucet or jet faces, and a ring at the waterline. Later you may notice weaker jets, a slower drain or a door gasket that feels stiff. Catching it early with a monthly wipe-down keeps it from building up.

Can I use vinegar to clean my walk-in tub?

Many owners use a diluted white vinegar solution for scale on surfaces and in some jet systems, but always check your tub's manual first. Some makers recommend a specific cleaner, and strong acids or abrasive pads can damage acrylic, seals and finishes. When in doubt, ask the manufacturer or call us.

Do I need a whole-house softener just for a walk-in tub?

No, a tub works without one. A softener helps the whole house, including the water heater and faucets, so many Arizona owners find it worthwhile. If a softener is not practical, choosing air jets and running cleaning cycles does most of the work.

Is Tucson water as hard as Phoenix water?

Tucson water is also considered hard, though the exact level depends on where your water comes from. Your utility's yearly water quality report lists the hardness for your area. Either way, the same care steps apply.
