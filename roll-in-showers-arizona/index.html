Who it is for

When a roll-in shower is the safer choice

A walk-in tub still has a small step and asks you to sit and stand from a seat. For many people that is fine. For others, any step is one step too many.

A roll-in shower is a shower with no curb at the entry. The floor of the shower meets the bathroom floor, so you can walk in, push a walker in, roll a wheelchair in, or be wheeled in on a shower chair by a caregiver. Nothing to lift your foot over and nothing to climb out of.

It is usually the better pick if you use a wheelchair, if you shuffle rather than lift your feet, if a caregiver helps you bathe, or if you simply prefer showers to baths. If you love a long soak, compare it with a walk-in tub with a built-in seat before you decide.

Shower safety matters because the bathroom is where many falls happen. More than two-thirds of nonfatal bathroom injuries happen in or near the tub or shower.

Source: CDC MMWR on bathroom injuries.

ADA vs. home

ADA showers and residential showers are not the same thing

You will see "ADA shower" in many ads. The ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act) sets exact size and grab bar rules for public places like hotels and clinics. A private home does not have to meet those rules.

That is good news. It means your shower can be built around your body instead of a rulebook. An ADA-style base is a sound choice if you want maximum space. But a residential base sized to your existing tub alcove is often just as usable and much easier to install.

What matters most at home is turning space. If you use a wheelchair, think about the whole path: the bathroom door, the space between the toilet and vanity, and whether you can turn to face the shower. Measure that clear floor area before you choose a base. We look at it with you, from photos or a visit, before anything is ordered.

If a caregiver helps you bathe, plan room for two people. A wider base, a seat that faces out toward the open side, and a handheld shower your helper can reach without stepping in all make bath time easier and calmer for both of you.

Tight spaces

Narrow doors and hallways? Panel kits solve that

Many Arizona homes have narrow bathroom doors, tight hallway turns or a small guest bath. A one-piece shower may not fit through. Shower kits often come in panels: the base and walls arrive in sections that carry through a normal doorway and join together in place.

Panels also mean less demolition. In many tub-to-shower conversions the old tub comes out, the panels go up over prepared walls, and the job is done in as little as one day. Paying for it

Why roll-in showers fit ALTCS and VA programs well

ALTCS (the Arizona Long Term Care System, part of AHCCCS) can cover home modifications that have a specific adaptive purpose, based on a needs assessment and written into your service plan. Roll-in showers and walk-in showers with handheld showerheads are the kind of bathroom change the policy lists. Walk-in tubs are not typically listed.

So if you are an ALTCS member, a roll-in shower is usually the option to ask your case manager about. Our step-by-step ALTCS roll-in shower guide explains the process. Veterans may also qualify for VA grants. See every option on the Arizona funding page.

FAQ

Roll-in showers, answered

Will water spill onto my bathroom floor with no curb?

A well-built roll-in shower slopes gently toward the drain, and many include a collapsible water dam or a trench drain at the entry. A weighted curtain or a half-height door also helps. Proper slope set by the installer is what keeps water in.

Can a roll-in shower replace my existing bathtub?

Yes. Many roll-in shower bases come in a 60-inch length, which fits the same space as a standard tub. The installer removes the tub, adjusts the drain and sets the new base and walls, often in as little as one day.

What is the difference between a roll-in shower and a walk-in shower?

A roll-in shower has no curb, so a wheelchair or rolling shower chair can go straight in. A walk-in shower usually has a small, low threshold that most people can step over but a wheelchair cannot cross easily.

Do I need a fold-down seat or a separate shower chair?

A wall-mounted fold-down seat stays put and folds away when others shower. A rolling shower chair lets a caregiver wheel you in and out. Many people use a fold-down seat; wheelchair users often prefer a rolling chair.

Is a roll-in shower easier to clean than a tub?

Usually, yes. Solid-surface or acrylic wall panels have few or no grout lines, so there is less place for hard water scale and mildew to settle. A quick wipe after showering keeps Arizona mineral spots down.

Can I add a roll-in shower on a concrete slab?

Yes. Many Arizona homes sit on a slab, and shower bases are made to work with them. The installer may need to recess the base or adjust the drain in the concrete to get a level, curbless entry, and that is listed in your quote.
