The basics

What every walk-in tub has in common

A walk-in tub is a deep bathtub with a watertight door in the side and a seat built in at about chair height. You step over a low threshold, sit down, close the door and fill the tub around you. When you are done, the water drains and you open the door.

That simple design removes the two hardest parts of a regular tub: climbing over a high wall and getting up from the floor of the tub. Most walk-in tubs fit the same 60 by 30 to 32 inch space as a standard tub, so many installs are a direct swap. Compact models around 48 to 53 inches long fit smaller bathrooms.

Where tubs differ is in the jets, the door, the width and the drain. Those choices decide how the tub feels every day and how well it holds up in Arizona. This page walks through each one. If you cannot step over even a few inches, or you use a wheelchair, a tub may not be the right tool. A curbless roll-in shower has no step at all.

  • Built-in seat at about chair height

Tub types

Four kinds of walk-in tubs, and who each one suits

Start with the type. Features come second.

Soaker

Warm, deep water and nothing else. Soakers have the fewest parts, cost the least and are the easiest to keep clean. A good fit if you mainly want a safe, relaxing bath.

Hydrotherapy

Adds air jets, water jets or both. Many people find warm water and jets soothing for sore joints and muscles. Ask your doctor about heat, blood pressure and circulation first.

Bariatric

A wider tub with a wider seat, a wider door and a higher weight rating. Built for larger frames, longer legs or anyone who wants more room to move.

Wheelchair transfer

An outward door that opens wide and a seat placed so you can slide sideways from a wheelchair. You still need to make a seated transfer, so test the motion first.

Jets and Arizona water

Air jets or water jets? Hard water helps you decide

Water jets (also called whirlpool jets) pull bath water through a pump and push it back out in strong streams. They feel like a firm massage. The catch is that some water stays in the pipes after you drain, so the system needs regular cleaning.

Air jets blow warm air through many small holes, which feels like gentle bubbles across your whole body. Most air systems blow the lines dry after each bath, so less water sits in them.

That difference matters here. Phoenix water runs roughly 158 to 344 milligrams of hardness per liter, about 9 to 20 grains per gallon. Hard water leaves white scale in jets, inline heaters, drain valves and door seals. Air jets tolerate it better, and a water softener plus a monthly cleaning cycle keep any tub working longer. Our guide to hard water and walk-in tubs covers the routine in detail.

Want both? Combination tubs offer air and water jets, so you choose the feel each day. Learn more about jet systems on the Aging Safely Baths hydrotherapy bathtub page.

Doors and drains

Inward or outward door, and why the drain matters

Inward-opening doors swing into the tub. They need no clear floor space in front, which helps in a tight bathroom, and the weight of the water presses the door against its seal. The trade-off is a narrower path in and out, because the door sits inside the tub when open.

Outward-opening doors swing into the room. The full opening is free for your legs, which is easier for stiff hips and for anyone moving from a wheelchair or walker. You need clear floor space where the door swings, so measure the distance to the toilet and vanity.

Drain speed is the feature buyers tell us they care about most after the first week. You sit in the tub while it drains, and a standard drain can take several minutes. A fast or dual drain uses two outlets, sometimes with a pump, to empty the tub much sooner. In a bathroom that is cooler than you are, that means less time getting chilled.

Ask how the drain works and how the tub drains if the power goes out.

Before you choose

Five things to check before you pick a model

  1. Your seat height. Measure the chair you find easiest to stand up from. Look for a tub seat close to that height.
  2. Your step-in height. Thresholds vary by model. If even a few inches feels risky, compare a shower instead.
  3. Your path to the bathroom. Many tubs are 26 to 32 inches wide, so doorways and hallway turns matter as much as the alcove.
  4. Your water heater. A deep tub needs enough hot water to fill it. If you run short during showers now, plan for that.
  5. Who else bathes here. A handheld shower and a fast drain keep the tub practical for a spouse, guests or a caregiver.

FAQ

Choosing a walk-in tub, answered

How deep is the water in a walk-in tub?

Many walk-in tubs hold water roughly up to your shoulders while you sit on the built-in seat, which is much deeper than a regular tub. The exact depth depends on the model and the seat height. Ask for the water depth from the seat on any model you compare.

Do I have to sit in the tub while it fills and drains?

Yes. The door must be closed before you fill and you open it after the water drains, so you sit inside for both. That is why a fast or dual drain and a warm seat or inline heater matter, especially if you get chilled easily.

What is the difference between an inward and an outward door?

An inward door swings into the tub, so it needs no room in front and water pressure helps seal it. An outward door swings into the bathroom, which gives more room to step in and helps with wheelchair transfers. Measure the space by your toilet and vanity before you choose.

How much weight can a walk-in tub hold?

Standard tubs are built for most adults, and bariatric models have wider seats, wider doors and a higher weight rating. Every model lists its own rating, so tell us your height and weight range and we will only suggest tubs rated for you.

Will a walk-in tub use a lot of hot water?

A walk-in tub usually holds more water than a standard tub, so your water heater needs enough capacity to fill it warm. Check your tank size before you order a deep soaker. An inline heater keeps water warm once the tub is full but does not fill it.

Can someone else use the tub as a regular shower?

Yes. Most models include a handheld shower on a slide bar, so the tub works as a seated or standing shower for everyone in the house. Ask for a long hose if a caregiver will help with bathing.
